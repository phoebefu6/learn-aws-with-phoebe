The bill you did not plan

Nobody signs off on a $15,000 query habit. It arrives one dashboard refresh at a time, on a layout chosen in a hurry, next to a handful of small line items nobody priced because each one looked like rounding. Tonight we read a data team's bill the way it is actually built: the scanning meter first, then the quiet minimums and fees underneath it, and finally the two switches, a budget alert and activated cost tags, that have to be on before the month starts if they are going to help at all.

Part 0

Where we start

Session 1 gave you one sentence: storing a terabyte in S3 Standard costs $23.55 a month, and scanning it as CSV a hundred times a day through Athena costs $15,000 (us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify before relying on either). Session 4 named the meters. Tonight we take the bill apart. Most unplanned spend on a data platform is not one big mistake. It is a layout that makes every question read too much, plus a few minimums and fees that are each tiny, plus nobody noticing until the invoice, because the alert and the tags were never switched on.

Part 1 · covers what a scan costs, by layout

The same question, three prices

5 min live

Athena bills $5.00 per terabyte scanned, "rounded up to the nearest megabyte, with a 10 MB minimum per query" (Athena pricing page; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026). So the price of a question is decided less by the question than by how the files underneath it are laid out. Analyst session 4 measured the bytes on 100,000 real New York taxi trips stored three ways, and analyst session 8 scaled them to a terabyte of CSV asked 100 times a day. The bytes are measured; the scaling is modelled.

LiveThree layouts, one question2 min▶

Take the one-week question: how many trips started in each pickup zone between 15 and 21 January. On raw CSV, Athena has to read every byte of every row, because a CSV file has no index and no columns it can skip: $5.00 a query, $15,000 a month at 100 queries a day. Convert the same data to one Parquet file and Athena reads only the columns it needs: $1,130 a month. Partition that Parquet by pickup day, so each day sits under its own key prefix, and Athena opens only the seven daily folders the question names: $88 a month. Same answer every time; analyst session 4 checked that the top three zones and their counts are identical across all three layouts.

All figures: measured bytes on the 100,000-row sample, scaled linearly to 1,024 GB of CSV and 100 queries a day for 30 days (modelled), at $5.00 per TB; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify before relying on them. Your numbers will differ, but the order of magnitude between the rows is the lesson.

LiveWhen partitions cost more2 min▶

Now ask a whole-month question: the average tip by payment type. The single Parquet file reads two columns once: $293 a month. The partitioned layout has to open all 31 daily files, and each small file carries its own footer and overhead, so it reads more: $566 a month (modelled, same prices and caveats). Partitioning is a bet on how people filter. It pays when most questions name a narrow slice of the partition key, and it costs when most questions span all of it.

Be honest about the size of this effect. On the sample each daily file is only 73,785 to 113,419 bytes, so the overhead is a large share of each one. Scaling linearly to a terabyte keeps that share constant, which overstates it: at real scale each day's file would be far larger and the fixed overhead a smaller fraction. The direction is real; the ratio at your scale needs measuring, not quoting.

Self-studyWhy the proof of concept looked cheap2 min▶

The whole sample is about 10 MB of CSV. Athena bills a minimum of 10 MB per query, so at proof-of-concept size every layout costs the same: $0.000048 a query (us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026). Raw CSV and partitioned Parquet are indistinguishable on the bill, and a team concludes "Athena is basically free". The minimum hides the difference until the data grows past it, and by then the dashboards are built on whatever layout the proof of concept used.

AWS's own claim is that you "can save up to 90% per query and get better performance by compressing, partitioning, and converting your data into columnar formats" (Athena pricing page). The measured sample does better than that on narrow questions and worse on wide ones: reading every column of the month from one Parquet file still costs $3,547 a month against CSV's $15,000 (modelled), a saving of about three quarters. Treat "up to 90%" as AWS's ceiling, not your forecast. Note too that Athena bills "the number of bytes scanned before decompression", so compression lowers the bill directly. Why columnar formats read less is a file-format question, taught in learn AI infra.

Real world

The BI tool that refreshed the raw folder. An analyst connects a BI tool to the quickest table to find, which is the raw CSV landing area, and sets the dashboard to refresh every few minutes so the numbers look live. Every refresh reads every byte, the question is about last week, and nobody sees a problem because the proof of concept ran on a few megabytes under the 10 MB minimum. The fix took one afternoon: point the dashboard at the partitioned Parquet table and refresh hourly. Constructed; the per-layout costs are the modelled figures above.

Part 2 · covers the quieter line items

The small print under every meter

6 min live

Scanning is the loud line on a data bill. Underneath each meter sits a rule that is reasonable on its own and expensive in bulk: a minimum per query, a minimum per object, a minimum per warehouse start, a fee per thousand objects, a free allowance that ends. None of them is hidden; all of them are on AWS's own pages. They become a surprise only because an estimate priced the headline unit and stopped.

LiveSmall files cost twice2 min▶

Athena's own guidance is blunt: "Datasets that consist of many small files result in poor overall query performance", and "For small files, the overhead of the columnar file format outweighs the benefits." You saw the bill side in Part 1: the partitioned sample split into 31 files of 73,785 to 113,419 bytes, and a whole-month question read nearly twice the bytes of the single file. The second cost is requests: every file is one more PUT when it is written, at $0.005 per 1,000, and one more GET each time it is read, at $0.0004 per 1,000 (us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify).

What AWS does not give is a target file size. You will hear "aim for 128 MB files" quoted as a rule; Athena's documentation gives 128 MB only as the default Parquet row group size, not as a file-size target, and these pages found no file-size number at source. Ask instead whether files are many, small and rarely compacted. How a team schedules compaction belongs to pipeline design, in learn data engineering.

LiveThe warehouse that bills while nobody is asking2 min▶

Redshift Serverless bills "in RPU-hours on a per-second basis, with a 60-second minimum charge", and nothing for compute while no queries run. That second half is real and is why it is attractive. The first half is the trap for scattered work: a 5-second query on an idle 8 RPU warehouse bills $0.0500, not the $0.0042 five seconds would cost (modelled in analyst session 6 at $0.375 per RPU-hour; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify). One query a minute, each waking an idle warehouse, pays for a full minute every time.

Two more lines from the same billing page. "Maintaining open connection pools can generate costs even when no actual user workloads are running": a BI tool that holds connections open overnight can keep the meter alive. And "If you run a query and cancel it before it finishes, you are still billed for the time the query ran." Athena has the same rule in its own unit: "Canceled queries are billed for the total amount of data scanned when the query is canceled." Failed queries and DDL statements, by contrast, are not charged on Athena.

Self-studyStorage minimums and the monitoring fee2 min▶

Cheaper storage classes carry floors. Standard-IA bills a minimum of 30 days and 128 KB per object: "If an object is less than 128 KB, Amazon S3 charges you for 128 KB", and an object deleted early is "charged for 30 days" (Amazon S3 User Guide, "Understanding and managing Amazon S3 storage classes"). Glacier Instant Retrieval's minimum is 90 days and Deep Archive's 180. A lifecycle rule that moves objects out early is "charged for the remainder of that duration". Session 2 drew the waterfall; the bill-side point is that a cheap class with a short-lived, small-object workload can cost more than Standard.

Intelligent-Tiering moves objects between tiers on its own and has no retrieval fees, in exchange for a monitoring and automation fee of $0.0025 per 1,000 objects a month (S3 pricing page; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify). Objects under 128 KB are not monitored and not eligible for auto-tiering. The fee is per object, not per gigabyte, so it is negligible on a few large files and noticeable on hundreds of millions of small ones. Since September 2024, lifecycle rules by default do not transition objects under 128 KB at all.

Self-studyData transfer: free in, free up to a point out1 min▶

"Data transferred in from the internet" is on S3's free list, and "AWS customers receive 100GB of data transfer out to the internet free each month, aggregated across all AWS Services and Regions (except China and GovCloud)." Past that, transfer out is charged per gigabyte. These pages quote no per-gigabyte rate for it, because none was verified for them: read the current rate on the S3 pricing page before you price a workload that sends data out, such as a partner extract or a dashboard tool hosted outside AWS. Network design beyond that is not covered on these pages.

Real world

The cheaper class that cost more. A team moves its event logs, millions of objects of a few kilobytes each, to Standard-IA to save money, and deletes them after a week. Every object is billed as 128 KB, and every deletion before day 30 is billed as 30 days. The class with the lower price per gigabyte produced the higher bill, and nobody had changed anything but a storage class. The fix was to batch the small files into larger ones before they landed and keep the short-lived prefix in Standard. Constructed; the minimums are from the S3 User Guide.

Part 3 · covers catching the bill before it arrives

An alert, a tag, and a rule that deletes

4 min live

The invoice is the slowest possible alarm. AWS gives a data leader three earlier ones, and each has a delay you have to plan around. AWS Budgets warns on spend, but its data refreshes only a few times a day. Cost allocation tags say whose spend it was, but only after someone activates them, which takes up to two days. And a lifecycle rule stops the storage line growing without anyone remembering to delete anything. All three work only if they were set up before the month you need them in.

LiveBudgets: actual and forecast2 min▶

AWS Budgets lets you "be alerted for both actual (after accruing) and forecasted (before accruing) spends". The actual alert tells you that money has gone. The forecast alert tells you where the month is heading while there is still time to act, which is the one a data leader should care about most: a dashboard reading raw CSV shows up as a steep forecast days before it shows up as a large actual.

Two caveats, both from AWS's own page. "AWS Budgets information is updated up to three times a day", so a runaway query can run for hours before the data the alert reads has moved. And "There can be a delay between when you incur a charge and when you receive a notification from AWS Budgets". AWS gives no number for that delay, so neither do these pages. Design for it: a budget is an early warning, not a circuit breaker.

LiveTags have to be switched on first1 min▶

A cost allocation tag is a key such as team or dataset on a resource, used to split the bill. Tagging the resource is not enough: "For tags to appear on your billing reports, you must activate them." And activation is slow by design: "it can take up to 24 hours for the tag keys to appear on your cost allocation tags page for activation. It can then take up to 24 hours for tag keys to activate." Up to two days, in other words, from tagging to a report that can answer "whose spend was that?". Activate the keys when the platform is built, not on the morning the bill arrives.

Self-studyLifecycle as a cost control1 min▶

The Well-Architected Data Analytics Lens, best practice 13.1: "If data is stored in Amazon S3, use Amazon S3 Lifecycle configurations to expire data automatically." Raw landing files that have been converted, query results nobody reopens, and intermediate outputs of a job are the usual candidates. The rule works without anyone remembering, and session 2 showed that no bucket policy can block it, so an expiration rule deserves the same review as a delete permission. The Lens was last updated on 22 December 2023, almost three years ago: read it as sound principle, not a current product list.

Combine it with the storage minimums from Part 2. AWS's own example rule moves objects to Standard-IA "30 days after creation"; that is an example, not a requirement, but it lines up with the 30-day minimum charge so an object is not billed for days it no longer exists.

Real world

The weekend nobody could attribute. A scheduled job starts rerunning a wide query in a loop on a Friday evening. The budget's actual alert lands on Saturday. On Monday the platform lead opens the cost report to find which team's job it was, and the tag column is empty: the keys had been added to every resource months earlier but never activated. Activating them that morning helped the next incident, not this one. Constructed; the delays are AWS's documented ones.

Build-along

A five-line cost guardrail plan

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No console and no account. One sheet, five lines, each with a setting, an owner and how often someone checks it. Every line answers one part of tonight: a budget that warns early, tags that say whose spend it was, a layout rule that keeps scans narrow, a lifecycle rule that keeps storage from piling up, and an idle check for the warehouse. The worked examples are constructed; your thresholds and your numbers will differ.

Line 1: a budget with a forecast alert. Pick the account or accounts the data platform runs in. Write a monthly figure you would be uncomfortable to exceed, an actual alert at that figure, and a forecast alert below it so someone hears while there is time to act. Name who receives it; a shared mailbox nobody reads is not an owner.

★ Worked example: line 1 (constructed)Budget: data platform account, monthly Actual: alert at 100% of the monthly figure Forecast: alert when the forecast passes 80% Owner: platform lead, plus the analytics lead in copy Caveat: data refreshes up to 3 times a day; alerts can lag

Line 2: the tag keys you will activate. Choose three or four keys that answer the questions you will ask of a bad month: which team, which dataset, which environment. Write the date you activated them and a date two days later to check they appear on the billing reports.

★ Worked example: line 2 (constructed)Keys: team, dataset, env Activated: Monday, on the cost allocation tags page Check: Wednesday (up to 24 h to appear + up to 24 h to activate) Owner: platform lead; every new resource gets all three keys

Line 3: one layout rule. Write the rule that would have prevented Part 1's $15,000 month. Base it on how your people actually filter, and include the check that stops it backfiring on whole-month questions.

★ Worked example: line 3 (constructed)Rule: no dashboard or scheduled query reads raw/ CSV Curated: Parquet, partitioned by the column most questions filter on Check: price one whole-period question too; if partitions make it dearer and it runs often, keep an unpartitioned table for it Owner: analytics lead

Line 4: one lifecycle rule. Pick the prefix that grows fastest and is reread least, usually the raw landing area once it has been converted. Write what happens to it and when, then check the timing against the storage minimums from Part 2.

★ Worked example: line 4 (constructed)Prefix: raw/ (already converted into curated/) Action: expire after 90 days Not: move to Standard-IA and delete within 30 days (billed 30 anyway) Note: objects under 128 KB are not transitioned by default Owner: data engineering lead; reviewed like a delete permission

Line 5: a warehouse idle check. If you run Redshift Serverless or plan to, write who looks, how often, at whether anything is keeping it busy without users: connection pools held open by a BI tool, many short queries each paying the 60-second minimum, cancelled queries that still billed their time.

★ Worked example: line 5 (constructed)Look at: usage in hours with no human users (nights, weekends) Ask: which tool held connections open? how many queries were under 60 s? how many were cancelled? Action: close idle pools; batch short scheduled queries together Owner: analytics lead, monthly
✗ The guardrail plan that arrives with the invoicebudget: "we'll set one up if costs get high" tags: added to resources, never activated layout: whatever the proof of concept used lifecycle: "storage is cheap, keep everything" warehouse: nobody owns the overnight hours

Price the question, then ask who hears first. For every estimate, ask two things: how many bytes does the most frequent question read, in the layout it will actually use, and who receives the forecast alert if that number is wrong. If either answer is "we'll see", the plan is not finished. Bring the sheet to session 6: it becomes the cost row on the one-page platform.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Find your most frequent question. Ask for the query or dashboard that runs most often against the lake, how often it runs, and how many bytes one run scans. Multiply by $5.00 per TB and the runs a month (us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify), and compare with what you assumed.
  2. Check whether your tags are active. Ask whoever manages billing whether your cost allocation tag keys are activated, not just applied. If they are not, activating them is the cheapest guardrail on the sheet.
  3. Find the existing budget. Find out whether a budget exists for your data platform's account, who receives its alerts, and whether it has a forecast alert or only an actual one.
  4. Finish the five lines with an owner each. Fill the sheet with the people who would own each line, and bring it: session 6 puts it on the one-page platform next to storage, catalog, engines and identity.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and leader session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A proof of concept on 10 MB of data showed raw CSV and partitioned Parquet costing exactly the same per Athena query. Why?

Athena bills bytes scanned "rounded up to the nearest megabyte, with a 10 MB minimum per query". Scaled to a terabyte asked 100 times a day, the same one-week question costs $15,000 a month on CSV and $88 partitioned (modelled; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026).

2 · Every resource in the data account carries a "team" tag, yet the cost reports show no team breakdown. What is the most likely reason?

"For tags to appear on your billing reports, you must activate them." Applying a tag to a resource is half the job. Activate the keys when the platform is built, because the delay is up to two days.

3 · Partitioning by day cut a one-week question from $1,130 to $88 a month, but a whole-month question went from $293 to $566. Why did partitioning make it dearer?

Partitioning pays when questions name a narrow slice of the key, and costs when they span it all. The answers were identical on every layout. Figures modelled from measured bytes; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify.
