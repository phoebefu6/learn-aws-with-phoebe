Part 0

Where we start

A platform diagram usually fails in one of two ways. It is a wall of logos with arrows, which says what was bought but not what it costs or who answers for it. Or it is a 40-page design document nobody outside the platform team reads. The one-page version sits between them: the handful of boxes a data team actually runs, and on each box exactly two facts, the meter it bills on and the owner. Session 1 drew the boxes. Sessions 2 to 5 gave each one its rules. Tonight we assemble them, check the drawing against AWS's own Well-Architected Data Analytics Lens, and turn the result into six questions you can put to any proposal.

Part 1 · covers the one-page platform

The whole platform, one drawing

5 min live

The Well-Architected Data Analytics Lens puts the founding idea in one line: "Decoupling storage from compute allows you to manage the cost of storage and compute separately" (best practice 11.1). That is the shape of the drawing. One lake in S3, split into zones by what the bytes are for. One catalog that says where everything is. Several engines that read the same files, each on its own meter. Identity wrapped around all of it, and a budget watching every meter at once. Bedrock sits beside the engines as one more reader of the curated zone.

LiveFour zones and one catalog2 min▶

The lake is one or a few S3 buckets split by key prefix into zones that each have one job. raw/ is where files land, as they arrived. curated/ is the cleaned, converted copy, Parquet partitioned by the column people filter on, and it is the zone every engine reads. results/ holds query outputs. archive/ is where a lifecycle rule sends what nobody rereads. Session 2 priced the floor: a terabyte in S3 Standard costs $23.55 a month, against $1.01 in Deep Archive (us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify before relying on either).

Beside the lake sits the Glue Data Catalog, which "stores metadata about your organization's data sets" and acts as "an index to the location, schema, and runtime metrics" (AWS Glue Developer Guide, "AWS Glue Data Catalog"). Athena, Redshift Spectrum and EMR all read the same catalog, which is why it is one box, not one per engine. It costs $1 per 100,000 objects a month beyond the free tier and $1 per million requests (us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify). The Lens calls a central catalog "an integral part of data governance" (best practice 7.1).

LiveThree engines, three meters2 min▶

Each engine is there because it bills differently, and session 4 sorted jobs by exactly that. Athena for ad hoc questions bills bytes: $5.00 per TB scanned with a 10 MB minimum per query, and nothing while nobody asks. Redshift Serverless for dashboards bills time: $0.375 per RPU-hour, per second with a 60-second minimum, and no compute charge while no queries run. Lambda for ingest bills requests and GB-seconds: $0.20 per million requests and $0.0000166667 per GB-second. All prices us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify before relying on them.

The meter decides what a habit costs. Modelled in analyst session 5: a Lambda converting one file a day for 20 seconds at 1 GB costs about $0.01 a month, one a minute about $14.41, and a Fargate container kept on for the same job $36.04. The shape matters more than the logo: the same engine can be the cheapest box on the page or the most expensive, depending on how often it runs.

Self-studyIdentity, budgets and the AI box2 min▶

Two bands run under every box, because they apply to all of them. IAM roles: every arrow on the drawing is a request made by some role, and session 3 showed that "By default, all requests are implicitly denied" and "An explicit deny overrides an explicit allow" (AWS IAM User Guide, "Policy evaluation logic"). Budgets and tags: one budget with an actual and a forecast alert watches every meter, and activated cost allocation tags say whose spend it was. Both bands are on the customer's side of the shared responsibility model, which AWS describes as security "in" the cloud.

Bedrock is "a fully managed service that provides secure, enterprise-grade access to high-performing foundation models" and "supports 100+ foundation models" (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, "What is Amazon Bedrock?"). On the drawing it is one more reader of curated/, with its own meter per thousand tokens. What a Claude model on Bedrock specifically offers is taught in learn Claude session 68; the inference infrastructure underneath belongs to learn AI infra.

Self-studyWhat is deliberately not on the page1 min▶

No streaming box: Kinesis and Kafka are taught in learn streaming data. No scheduler box: orchestration has its own home in learn data orchestration. No model of facts and dimensions inside Redshift: that is learn data warehouse. EMR, SageMaker, Lake Formation permissions, Iceberg tables, VPC design and cross-account access are not covered on these pages either. If a proposal you are reviewing leans on one of them, the six questions in Part 3 still apply; the detail behind the answers needs its own study.

Real world

Two proposals, one priced by storage only. Two vendors pitch the same analytics platform for a terabyte of trip data. The first quotes $23.55 a month: a terabyte in S3 Standard, which is accurate and is the whole quote. The second draws the one-page platform, puts a meter on every box, and prices the busiest dashboard question at 100 runs a day: $15,000 a month if it reads raw CSV, $88 if it reads curated Parquet partitioned by day (modelled from measured bytes; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026). The cheaper-looking proposal had simply stopped at the cheapest meter. Constructed; the figures are the modelled canon from analyst sessions 4 and 8, and your numbers will differ.

Part 2 · covers the Well-Architected Data Analytics Lens

Six pillars, read as principles

5 min live

AWS's Well-Architected Data Analytics Lens describes itself as "a collection of customer-proven best practices for designing well-architected analytics workloads". It is organised into six pillars, and it is the document a reviewer will reach for to check a drawing like yours. Use it, but read the date first: its last major update is December 22, 2023, first published May 20, 2020, and no later revision is listed. On 3 October 2026 that makes it almost three years old. Its principles have aged well; its product details have not been kept current, so check those against today's service pages.

LiveSix pillars, one date2 min▶

The six pillars are operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization and sustainability. They are the right six questions to ask of any architecture, and they do not go out of date. What does go out of date is the detail underneath them. The Lens's revision history shows a major update on December 22, 2023 and its first publication on May 20, 2020, and nothing since. So read it the way you would read a well-regarded textbook from three years ago: trust the reasoning, check every product name and limit against the service's current page.

The useful habit is the one in the figure: for each pillar, point at the box on your one-page platform where it bites first. If a pillar has nowhere to land, either the drawing is missing a box or the proposal has not thought about that pillar at all. The pairings above are our reading, not the Lens's; your platform may pair them differently, and that is fine as long as each one lands somewhere.

LiveFive best practices, five boxes2 min▶

Five of the Lens's best practices map straight onto the drawing, and each repeats a lesson from the leader track. BP 11.1: "Decoupling storage from compute allows you to manage the cost of storage and compute separately", which is why the lake and the engines are separate boxes with separate meters. BP 10.4, partition your data: "a full table scan is avoided, meaning faster performance and lower query cost", the $15,000 to $88 drop from session 5. BP 7.1: a central catalog is "an integral part of data governance". BP 5.2: least privilege works "by giving only enough access for systems to do the job". BP 13.1: "use Amazon S3 Lifecycle configurations to expire data automatically".

One more line is worth carrying into a cost review: "Using Parquet over CSV format can reduce storage costs significantly" (BP 13.1.6). The measured sample agrees: the same 100,000 trips took 23.6% of the CSV's bytes as a single Parquet file. Least privilege as a principle, and how to run access reviews around it, is taught in learn data access control; these pages stay with how AWS enforces it.

Self-studyWhat a three-year-old lens cannot tell you1 min▶

Several things a data leader relies on have moved on AWS's own pages, and a 2023 document cannot reflect the ones that changed after it. Since September 2024, lifecycle rules by default do not transition objects under 128 KB. Bedrock's original Agents feature is now called "Agents Classic" and "is no longer open to new customers", with AgentCore as its successor. Lambda's asynchronous payload limit is now 1 MB, not the older 256 KB. These pages do not date every one of those changes, and that is the point: the Lens tells you what to care about, and only the current service page tells you the current number.

Real world

Green on every pillar. An architecture review scores a proposal against the six pillars and marks all six green. Security is green because "we use IAM roles". Nobody asked how many actions the analyst role could take, and a single wildcard Allow had lifted it from 7 of 24 data-team actions to 22 of 24, the count analyst session 8 measured. The pillar was named and never tied to a box. The fix was the habit in the figure: every green needs a box and a number beside it. Constructed; the 7 and 22 of 24 are the measured canon from analyst session 8.

Part 3 · covers six questions to ask any AWS data proposal

Six questions that only take a number for an answer

5 min live

A proposal is ready to sign when it answers six questions with a name or a number, not with "it depends" or "we'll optimise later". Each question comes from one session of the leader track, and each has a figure on these pages you can hold the answer against. Ask them in order: the earlier answers change the later ones, because the Region sets the prices and the layout sets the bytes that every meter downstream bills on.

LiveQuestions 1 to 3: where, what layout, who2 min▶

1 · Which Region, and why? AWS runs 39 Regions with 124 Availability Zones, and "An AWS Region is a physical location in the world where we have multiple Availability Zones." Prices differ by Region, and every price on these pages is us-east-1 only. Some limits differ too: Redshift Serverless allows a base capacity up to 1,024 RPUs in only five Regions, us-east-1 among them, and 512 elsewhere. Which Bedrock models are offered also varies, so check the model you need is available where your data lives. A pass is one named Region and a reason: where the users are, where the data must stay, or which service is offered there.

2 · Where do the bytes live, and in what layout? Ask for the zones and the format of the zone the engines read. Then ask what share of the raw bytes a typical question reads, because that share is the bill. On the measured sample a single Parquet file was 23.6% of the CSV's bytes, and the one-week question read 0.58% of them once the Parquet was partitioned by day (analyst session 4). A pass names the zones, the layout and the bytes the busiest question reads.

3 · Who can touch what, and which explicit Denies are the floor? Ask for the roles, how many actions each can take, and which Denies no Allow can override. The measured warning from analyst session 8: one "Action": "*", "Resource": "*" Allow lifted the analyst role from 7 of 24 data-team actions to 22 of 24, while the two explicit Denies, DeleteBucket and PutBucketPolicy, still held. A pass lists the roles and names the Denies.

LiveQuestions 4 and 5: the meter and the bill later2 min▶

4 · Which meter does each engine run on, and what does it cost at our query volume? This is the question a storage-only quote never reaches. Analyst session 6 modelled one 20 GB question on Athena against an 8 RPU Redshift Serverless warehouse: at 10 a day Athena costs $34.87 a month, at 100 a day $298.54; the warehouse costs $95.81 busy one hour a day and $365.81 busy four. The crossovers fall at about 31 and 123 queries a day, depending on how many hours the warehouse is busy (modelled volumes; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify). A pass is a monthly price at your query count, not a price per unit.

5 · What will we not see on the bill until later? Ask the proposal to name session 5's quiet line items: the Athena 10 MB minimum per query, the Redshift Serverless 60-second minimum and open connection pools, the Standard-IA 30-day and 128 KB minimums, many small files, transfer out past the free 100 GB a month, and the lag in Budgets data, "updated up to three times a day". A pass names each one and who watches it.

Self-studyQuestion 6: the AI box2 min▶

6 · For AI work: which model, batch or on-demand, and what happens to our data? Bedrock offers batch inference "at a 50% lower price compared to on-demand", for prompts that can be answered asynchronously. Analyst session 7 modelled one million support notes at 120 input and 8 output tokens each on Nova Micro: $5.32 on-demand, $2.66 as a batch (modelled volumes; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify). A summary nobody reads until morning does not need on-demand prices.

Then the data question, and teach both halves of AWS's answer. Model providers "don't have access to Amazon Bedrock logs or to customer prompts and completions", and "your content is not used to improve the base models and is not shared with any model providers". But for some models, prompts and completions "are retained within the AWS boundary for up to 30 days and may be reviewed by AWS" (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, "Data retention"). A pass names the model, the mode, and confirms someone has read the retention terms for that specific model. Least privilege for the AI box is bedrock:InvokeModel on one foundation-model ARN, not bedrock:*.

Real world

The summariser that went back to legal. A team proposes summarising every support note overnight with a foundation model. The cost line is easy once someone asks question 6: as a batch job it is half the on-demand price, $2.66 against $5.32 per million notes on Nova Micro (modelled). The data line takes longer. The proposal said "Bedrock does not keep our data"; the chosen model was one AWS lists as retaining prompts and completions for up to 30 days. Nothing was wrong with the choice, but legal needed to know before, not after. Constructed; the prices are modelled from the Price List and the retention caveat is AWS's.

Build-along

Your data platform on one page

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No console and no account. One page, landscape. Start from the map you drew in session 1, then give every box the two facts it is missing, its meter and its owner, and finish with the two rows that make the page useful in a review: security and cost. The worked example is constructed on the taxi-data platform from these pages; your boxes, owners and numbers will differ.

Step 1: draw the boxes from session 1. Take the map where you placed your team's current tools onto AWS boxes. Keep only the boxes your platform actually runs or will run this year: storage zones, catalog, each engine, ingest, and the AI box if there is one. Draw the arrows the data takes. If a box has no arrow in or out, ask why it is on the page.

★ Worked example: the boxes (constructed)Sources -> Lambda ingest -> S3 raw/ S3 raw/ -> curated/ (Parquet, partitioned by pickup day) curated/ -> Athena (ad hoc), Redshift Serverless (dashboards), Bedrock (support-note summaries) Glue Data Catalog indexes curated/; Athena writes to results/ Lifecycle: raw/ expires after 90 days; old curated/ to archive/

Step 2: put a meter on every box. Use session 4's four meters: time, bytes, requests, storage. Write the unit and the list price beside each box, with the Region and the date you read it. A box you cannot put a meter on is either free, which you should confirm, or not understood yet.

★ Worked example: the meters (constructed)S3 zones storage $0.023 per GB-month, Standard Glue Data Catalog storage $1 per 100,000 objects a month Athena bytes $5.00 per TB scanned, 10 MB minimum Redshift Serverl. time $0.375 per RPU-hour, 60 s minimum Lambda ingest requests $0.20 per 1M + GB-seconds Bedrock tokens Nova Micro $0.000035 per 1K input All: us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify

Step 3: put an owner on every box. One named person or role per box: the one who is asked when its meter jumps. "The platform team" is not an owner. Leave a slot blank rather than invent a name; a blank owner is the most useful finding the page can produce.

★ Worked example: the owners (constructed)S3 zones, Glue catalog data engineering lead Athena analytics lead Redshift Serverless ________ (nobody owns the overnight hours) Lambda ingest ________ Bedrock ________ (who read the retention terms?)

Step 4: add the security row from analyst session 8. Under the boxes, write each role and how many of your team's common actions it can take, then the explicit Denies that are the floor. Check for any "Action": "*" Allow: on the bench a single wildcard took the analyst role from 7 of 24 actions to 22 of 24. Copy your access matrix from session 3 if you have it.

★ Worked example: the security row (constructed)Analyst role: 7 of 24 data-team actions allowed Wildcard check: none found (with one, the bench measured 22 of 24) Floor: explicit Deny on s3:DeleteBucket, s3:PutBucketPolicy AI box: bedrock:InvokeModel on one foundation-model ARN only Owner: platform lead; reviewed quarterly

Step 5: add the cost row from session 5. Take your busiest question or dashboard, its runs a day and the bytes one run reads in the layout it actually uses, and write the monthly price. Put your five-line guardrail plan beside it: the budget, the tags, the layout rule, the lifecycle rule and the idle check, each already with an owner.

★ Worked example: the cost row (constructed)Busiest: one-week pickups dashboard, 100 runs a day Layout: curated/ Parquet partitioned by day Monthly: $88 (the same on raw CSV: $15,000) - modelled Storage: $23.55 a month for a terabyte in Standard Guardrail: budget with 80% forecast alert, tags team/dataset/env active All: us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify
✗ The one-page platform that is really a logo wallboxes: every service the vendor sells, no arrows meters: "pay as you go" owners: "the platform team" security: "IAM is configured" cost: "$23.55 a month for storage"

Read the page in a review, not just draw it. Before the next platform decision, put the one-page drawing on the table and walk the six questions from Part 3 against it. Every question should land on a box or a row with a number beside it. The questions that land on blank space are the agenda for the meeting. Keep the page next to the Lens: the Lens tells you which pillars to check, the page tells you which box to point at.

Homework

After the leader track

30 min
  1. Fill the blank owners. For every box on your page with no owner, find the person who would be asked if its meter doubled next month, and ask whether they agree to be named. Write the date they agreed.
  2. Run the six questions on a real proposal. Take the last AWS proposal or estimate your team received and write one line per question: the answer it gave, or "not answered". Count the gaps.
  3. Read the Lens with a date in mind. Open the Well-Architected Data Analytics Lens, find one best practice that names a product or a limit, and check it against that service's current page. Note whether it still holds.
  4. Price your AI box. If your team runs or plans AI work on Bedrock, write the model, whether the work could run as a batch, and whether anyone has read that model's data retention terms. If nobody has, that is the next action, with an owner.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and leader session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · How should a data leader read the Well-Architected Data Analytics Lens in October 2026?

The Lens was first published May 20, 2020 and last majorly updated December 22, 2023, with no later revision listed. Its pillars and best practices, such as decoupling storage from compute (BP 11.1), still hold; product details such as limits have moved since.

2 · A proposal prices your platform at $23.55 a month: a terabyte in S3 Standard. Which question should you ask first?

Storage is the cheapest meter on the page. The same question costs $15,000 a month on raw CSV and $88 on partitioned Parquet (modelled from measured bytes; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify). A storage-only quote has simply stopped before the meter that matters.

3 · A team says "Bedrock never keeps our prompts". What is the accurate answer?

AWS states both halves: providers "don't have access to Amazon Bedrock logs or to customer prompts and completions", and for some models content is "retained within the AWS boundary for up to 30 days and may be reviewed by AWS" (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, "Data retention"). Read the terms for the specific model.
