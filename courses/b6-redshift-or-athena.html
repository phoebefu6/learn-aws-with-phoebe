Redshift or Athena

Two engines can answer the same SQL question over the same taxi trips, and they send two completely different kinds of bill. Athena charges for the bytes each query reads. Redshift Serverless charges for warehouse capacity, by the second, while queries run, and nothing for compute when they do not. Tonight we put both meters side by side, model the point where one gets cheaper than the other, and find the assumption that decides it. Spoiler: it is not the price list.

Part 0

Where we start

Session 4 measured what one question costs on Athena: the average tip by payment type reads 1.96% of the CSV's bytes from a single Parquet file, which is about 20 GB if the CSV were a terabyte. Tonight that 20 GB becomes the Athena side of a comparison. The other side is Redshift Serverless, a warehouse you never size by hand but which bills by time instead of bytes. Neither engine runs on a laptop, so the build-along is a cost model: real prices, a real measured scan size, and one assumption about how busy the warehouse is that you set yourself and must defend.

Part 1 · covers two billing models

One meter counts bytes, the other counts busy seconds

5 min live

Athena charges $5.00 per TB scanned, per query. Redshift Serverless charges $0.375 per RPU-hour, and its billing page is precise about when: "When queries run, you're billed according to the capacity used in a given duration, in RPU hours on a per-second basis." Then the line that makes it serverless: "When no queries are running, you aren't billed for compute capacity." Storage is a separate meter on both sides. All prices here are us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; prices change without notice, so re-verify before relying on them.

LiveTwo meters, one question3 min▶

Athena prices each query on its own: bytes scanned, "rounded up to the nearest megabyte, with a 10 MB minimum per query", at $5.00 per TB. Two analysts running the same query pay twice. A query that reads nothing much costs almost nothing. Session 4 showed how layout moves that number.

Redshift Serverless prices time. "You pay for the workloads you run in RPU-hours on a per-second basis, with a 60-second minimum charge." An RPU is a unit of warehouse capacity, and "One RPU provides 16 GB of memory." While any query is running, the warehouse bills the capacity it is using, second by second, at $0.375 per RPU-hour. Ten queries that overlap inside the same busy minute share that minute; ten queries spread across ten hours keep it busy for ten separate stretches. When nothing runs, compute bills nothing.

So the two engines answer different questions about your workload. Athena asks "how many bytes, how many times?" Redshift Serverless asks "how many seconds was anything running, and at what capacity?" The same team can get a cheap answer from one and an expensive answer from the other, and the build-along shows exactly where they swap.

Self-studyWhat base capacity sets, and why the default matters2 min▶

Base capacity is the RPU count a workgroup starts queries with. The capacity page: "The default base capacity for Amazon Redshift Serverless is 128 RPUs." It can be set "from 4 RPUs to 512 RPUs", and only "to 4 RPUs, or in units of 8 at or above 8 RPUs (8,16,24...512)". Five Regions, us-east-1 among them, allow up to 1,024. Because billing is RPU-hours, the RPU count multiplies every busy second: the default is sixteen times the 8 RPU the build-along assumes. Whether 8 RPU is enough for your queries is a performance question these pages do not answer; the console's query monitoring does. What these pages do answer is that capacity and time multiply, so halving either halves the compute line.

Self-studyStorage is a third meter, on both sides1 min▶

The Redshift billing page: "Storage is billed by GB / month. Storage billing is separate from billing for compute capacity." Managed storage is $0.024 per GB-month; S3 Standard, where Athena's data lives, is $0.023 for the first 50 TB (both us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify). The two are close enough that storage rarely decides the choice. The build-along includes it anyway, so the comparison is a whole monthly bill and not just the compute part: the Parquet copy of a terabyte of CSV is 242.1 GB, which is $5.57 a month in S3 and $5.81 in managed storage.

Real world

The month-end crunch. A finance team runs almost nothing for three weeks, then hammers the same tables for two days at month end. On Athena those two days cost exactly the bytes they read, and the quiet weeks cost only storage. On Redshift Serverless the quiet weeks also cost only storage, because an idle warehouse bills no compute, and the crunch costs however many seconds the warehouse stayed busy. Neither engine punishes the quiet weeks; they differ only in what they count during the crunch. The scenario is constructed; both billing rules are quoted from AWS's pricing and billing pages.

Part 2 · covers the modelled break-even

Where the lines cross, and what moves the crossing

6 min live

The build-along prices one Q2-style query, 20 GB scanned, on Athena at five daily volumes, and prices an 8 RPU Redshift Serverless warehouse that is busy one hour or four hours a day. Every row is modelled: real prices, a scan size scaled from session 4's measurement, and busy hours that are an assumption, not a measurement. Athena's line rises with every query. Redshift's lines are flat, because the model holds busy hours fixed. Where they cross is the break-even.

LiveRead the two bills3 min▶

At 20 GB a query and $5.00 per TB, one Athena run costs just under ten cents. A month of it, plus $5.57 to store the Parquet in S3, comes to:

  • 10 queries a day: $34.87 a month.
  • 50 a day: $152.05. 100 a day: $298.54.
  • 300 a day: $884.47. 1,000 a day: $2,935.26.

The 8 RPU warehouse, plus managed storage, costs $95.81 a month if it is busy one hour a day and $365.81 if it is busy four. The script solves for the crossing: about 31 queries a day against the one-hour warehouse, about 123 against the four-hour one. Below the crossing Athena is cheaper; above it the warehouse is. All of this is us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026, and modelled: re-verify the prices, and expect your numbers to differ.

LiveBusy hours decide it, and nobody prints them for you3 min▶

The price list did not change between the two break-evens. The only thing that changed was one number you typed: how many hours a day the warehouse is busy. Four times the hours, about four times the break-even. That is the honest headline of the whole comparison: whichever engine is "cheaper" depends on an assumption about your workload's shape, and the assumption is worth more scrutiny than either price.

The model also holds busy hours flat while the query count rises, which real warehouses do not do. Fifty more queries a day either fit inside minutes the warehouse was already busy, and cost nothing extra, or land in quiet hours and wake it up. Clustered queries favour the warehouse; scattered ones favour Athena. Before you trust either line, find out when your queries actually run.

Self-studyWhat the model leaves out2 min▶

It assumes 8 RPU is enough to run the queries in the hours given, which is a performance claim nobody has measured here. It charges the warehouse only for managed storage, as if the data lived only there; if the lake copy stays in S3 too, add its $5.57. It prices one query shape, and real dashboards mix shapes. It ignores the 60-second minimum and the traps in Part 3, all of which push the warehouse's real bill above its line. And it says nothing about how the warehouse's tables should be modelled once data is loaded: facts, dimensions and marts are taught in learn-data-warehouse. Treat the break-evens as a way to ask the right question, not as an answer to it.

Real world

The comparison that assumed one busy hour. A team counts about 80 dashboard queries a day, sees that the modelled break-even against a one-hour warehouse is about 31, and moves to Redshift Serverless. The dashboards refresh at different times across a ten-hour working day, so the warehouse is rarely idle for long. The bill lands far above the one-hour line, because the assumption, not the price, was wrong. Running the script with the busy hours their query log actually showed would have told them before the move. The scenario is constructed; your numbers will differ.

Part 3 · covers the traps, and why it need not be either-or

Three ways the warehouse meter outlives your query

4 min live

Athena's traps are about bytes: the wrong layout, the unpartitioned filter. Redshift Serverless's traps are about seconds, and the billing page names them itself. A short query is billed as a minute. An open connection can count as activity. A cancelled query is billed for the time it ran. None of them is a bug; all three are written down; most teams meet them on the invoice first.

LiveThe 60-second minimum2 min▶

The billing page: "The minimum charge is for 60 seconds of resource usage, metered on a per-second basis." So a 5-second query on an idle 8 RPU warehouse is billed for 60 seconds: $0.0500, not the $0.0042 its five seconds would suggest. The build-along prints both (modelled; us-east-1, AWS Price List, 3 October 2026; re-verify). One such query is pocket change. A health check, a dashboard tile or a scheduler ping that fires every few minutes into an otherwise idle warehouse turns pocket change into a line item, because each one buys a full minute. Athena has a floor too, the 10 MB minimum, but at $0.000048 a query it is a much quieter floor.

LiveConnection pools and cancelled queries2 min▶

Two sentences from the same billing page belong on every warehouse owner's wall. First: "maintaining open connection pools can generate costs even when no actual user workloads are running." A BI tool or notebook that holds connections open overnight can keep the meter alive with nobody asking anything. Second: "If you run a query and cancel it before it finishes, you are still billed for the time the query ran." Athena has the byte version of the same rule: cancelled queries are billed for the data they scanned. On both engines, cancel limits the damage; it does not undo it. How much an open pool actually costs on your workgroup is not something these pages measured; the billing page only says it can.

Self-studyBoth, not either: the lake stays in S32 min▶

The Data Analytics Lens, best practice 11.1: "Decoupling storage from compute allows you to manage the cost of storage and compute separately." Because the curated Parquet stays in S3 and is described once in the Glue Data Catalog, choosing Redshift Serverless for one workload does not force it on the others. The Glue documentation lists Athena and Redshift Spectrum among the services that read the catalog. A common shape is Athena for occasional, scattered questions, and a warehouse for the dense, repeated ones that keep it busy enough to earn its line. Remember the Lens dates from December 2023, so check current guidance before quoting it as policy. Which tables to load and how to model them belongs to learn-data-warehouse; the pipelines that load them belong to learn-data-engineering.

Real world

The dashboard that never logged off. A team moves its sales dashboards onto Redshift Serverless, and the daytime compute line looks exactly like the model. The overnight line does not look like zero. The dashboard tool keeps a connection pool open to the workgroup around the clock, and the billing page says that can generate cost with no user workload running. Setting the tool's pool to close idle connections, and a usage limit on the workgroup, brings the night back to quiet. The scenario is constructed; the billing behaviour is quoted from Redshift's billing page, and its size on any real workgroup was not measured here.

Build-along

Both bills in twenty lines

★ 22 min · everyone builds

One Python file, no imports, under a second to run. It holds four prices from the AWS Price List, one scan size from session 4, and one assumption you control: how busy the warehouse is. It prints Athena's monthly bill at five volumes, the warehouse's bill at two busy levels, the two break-evens, and the 60-second minimum in dollars. The output below is pasted from a real run of exactly this code. Before you run it, guess the break-even for a warehouse busy one hour a day.

Reuse the environment from analyst session 1. Same folder, same .venv. This script imports nothing, so there is nothing new to install and no files to read; it does not need the out/ folder. It was run on Python 3.11.11.

★ Step 1 · setup (verified 3 October 2026). .venv/bin/activate python --version # 3.10 or newer; verified on 3.11.11

The prices and the two inputs. Four prices from the Price List: Athena per TB, Redshift Serverless per RPU-hour, managed storage and S3 Standard per GB-month. GB_PER_QUERY is session 4's Q2 share of the CSV (1.96%) at 1 TB. STORED_GB is the Parquet copy of that terabyte.

★ Step 2 · b6_redshift_or_athena.py, part 1# Analyst session 6 - Redshift Serverless or Athena: a lake bills bytes, a warehouse bills time. # Prices: us-east-1, AWS Price List, retrieved 2026-10-03. Bytes: measured in session 4. # The warehouse side is MODELLED: how long your queries keep RPUs busy is an assumption you set. ATHENA_TB, RPU_HR, RMS_GB_MO, S3_GB_MO = 5.00, 0.375, 0.024, 0.023 GB_PER_QUERY = 20.0 # Q2 on 1 TB of CSV-equivalent data, stored as one Parquet file (session 4) STORED_GB = 242.1 # that table as Parquet

Two monthly bills. Athena: queries a day, times 30 days, times the GB each reads, priced per TB, plus S3 storage. Redshift Serverless: busy hours a day, times 30, times the RPU count, priced per RPU-hour, plus managed storage. Notice what is missing from redshift_month: the number of queries. That is the model's biggest assumption, written as code.

★ Step 3 · b6_redshift_or_athena.py, part 2def athena_month(queries_per_day): return queries_per_day * 30 * GB_PER_QUERY / 1024 * ATHENA_TB + STORED_GB * S3_GB_MO def redshift_month(busy_hours_per_day, base_rpu=8): return busy_hours_per_day * 30 * base_rpu * RPU_HR + STORED_GB * RMS_GB_MO

Print the table, solve the break-evens, price the minimum. The break-even line subtracts Athena's fixed storage from the warehouse bill and divides by what one more daily query costs Athena a month. The last line prices the 60-second minimum against what five seconds would have cost.

★ Step 4 · b6_redshift_or_athena.py, part 3print(f"{'queries/day':>11s} {'Athena $/mo':>12s} | warehouse busy 1 h/day at 8 RPU: ${redshift_month(1):,.2f}/mo, 4 h/day: ${redshift_month(4):,.2f}/mo") for q in (10, 50, 100, 300, 1000): print(f"{q:>11d} {athena_month(q):>12,.2f}") for busy in (1, 4): cross = (redshift_month(busy) - STORED_GB * S3_GB_MO) / (30 * GB_PER_QUERY / 1024 * ATHENA_TB) print(f"break-even with a warehouse busy {busy} h/day at 8 RPU: about {cross:,.0f} queries a day") print("60-second minimum: a 5-second query on an idle warehouse bills", f"${8 * 60 / 3600 * RPU_HR:.4f}", "at 8 RPU, not", f"${8 * 5 / 3600 * RPU_HR:.4f}")

Run it and compare with this real output. This one has no measurement in it, so your numbers match exactly unless you changed an input. Check the first line: $95.81 and $365.81.
